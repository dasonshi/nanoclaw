# Email Format Guide

## Structure
- Open with context acknowledgment -- if there's a communication gap, name it directly. "I sent X, never heard back, wasn't sure on our status" > pretending the gap doesn't exist.
- If no gap, open with one direct line that gets to the point. No "Hope you're doing well."
- Use bullet lists or short sections when presenting multiple items (data needs, pricing, specs). Emails get scanned, not read word-by-word.
- Use inline transitions to introduce sections: "Here's my proposal:", "Besides this,", "I'm recommending the following:" -- NOT bold standalone headers like **What I'm recommending** or **Proposal -- two pieces**. David's emails read like a person writing, not a slide deck.
- Close with a clear next step or ask. Include next-step signals that show awareness of the client's process: "If this sounds amenable, I can draft a formal quote for you to pass along" > "Let me know your thoughts."

## Formatting rules
- Em-dashes: use `--` (double hyphen, no spaces), NOT unicode. Max 2-3 per email.
- Paragraphs: 2-4 sentences each. No walls of text.
- Bullets: plain, no sub-bullets unless truly nested. Start with the noun/thing, not a verb phrase.
- Bold sparingly -- avoid bolding prices, totals, or section headers. Keep prices inline with line items. "Architecture restructure: $1,000" not "Architecture restructure -- **$1,000**"
- Sign-off: match the relationship. "David" for warm contacts, "Best, David" or "Best\nDavid" for new ones. Never "Cheers" or "Warmly."
- Compliments work better as parentheticals than standalone sentences: "(really well organized -- made it easy to map everything out, thanks)" > "Really well organized. Made it easy to see how the system is wired."

## Tone calibration
- Professional but human. Like you're talking to a smart colleague, not writing a memo.
- OK to show personality -- a quick aside, a real reaction. Not performative.
- Questions land better than demands. "Would you want X or Y?" > "Please provide X."
- Don't over-explain. Trust the reader to be smart.
- Compress problem descriptions. "It's messy to manage" > three sentences explaining why it's messy. The reader lives the problem -- they don't need the full thesis.
- Use "for now" scoping language when framing a recommendation that might evolve.
- Drop technical jargon in client-facing proposals. "Getting access" not "getting a Private Integration Token via the API." The client doesn't need to know the mechanism.

## What to avoid
- "Just wanted to follow up" / "Just checking in" -- say what you actually want
- "Per my last email" energy
- Excessive hedging: "I was wondering if maybe possibly..."
- Generic closers: "Happy to chat anytime!" / "Don't hesitate to reach out"
- Over-structured formatting that looks like a consulting deck -- too many bold headers, bolded prices, numbered sections with bold labels
- Technical jargon when plain language works ("access" not "API token", "setup" not "architecture audit via read-only scopes")
